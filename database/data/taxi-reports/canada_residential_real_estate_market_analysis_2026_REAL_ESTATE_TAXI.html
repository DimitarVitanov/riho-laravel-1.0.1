02 · DIRECT MARKET ANSWER

Canada is stabilizing, but it is not one market

The national market moved closer to balance in June 2026. Sales increased modestly, the national MLS Home Price Index stopped falling month over month for the first time since January 2025, and inventory remained close to its long-run norm. Nevertheless, the HPI was still 3.6% below June 2025, and expensive markets remain more price-sensitive than many lower-priced provinces.

The central investment conclusion is not “Canada will rise” or “Canada will fall.” It is that regional selection, property type, financing structure, tax status and local regulation now matter more than the national headline. Ontario and British Columbia offer more negotiating leverage in several segments, while parts of Quebec, the Prairies and Atlantic Canada have shown stronger price resilience. Rental vacancies have risen, reducing the probability that aggressive asking-rent growth can be repeated without careful submarket selection.

MARKET EVIDENCE

Prices, Transactions, Liquidity & Regional Structure

The report separates official and industry transaction data from asking-price indicators, forecasts and Real Estate Taxi inference.

01

1. How to read the data: four evidence layers

Canadian housing statistics can appear contradictory because they measure different things. This report keeps four evidence layers separate:

  • Official or regulated public data: Statistics Canada indexes, CMHC construction and rental surveys, federal and provincial tax rules, and Bank of Canada policy data.
  • Transaction-system data: CREA and local real estate board MLS statistics, including average sale prices, benchmark indexes, sales and listings.
  • Asking-price or portal estimates: advertised purchase prices, advertised rents and calculated gross yields. These are useful for screening but are not completed transaction evidence.
  • Real Estate Taxi analysis: interpretation, risk ranking, scenario design and investment implications. These are analytical judgments, not guarantees.
A national average sale price can rise even when a quality-adjusted price index falls, because the mix of homes and regions sold in a month changes. For valuation, a local benchmark and comparable completed sales are usually more useful than the national average.

03

3. Nominal versus inflation-adjusted price performance

Global Property Guide, using the Teranet–National Bank repeat-sales index, reported a 5.03% nominal year-over-year decline in March 2026 and an estimated 7.24% inflation-adjusted decline. Its May narrative reported that the same 11-city repeat-sales index was down 4.3% year over year. CREA’s broader MLS HPI showed a smaller 3.6% annual decline in June.

The difference is not necessarily an error. Teranet tracks repeat sales of single-family homes in selected metropolitan areas, while CREA’s HPI covers a wider MLS geography and different property baskets. Real returns also depend on inflation, transaction costs, financing and tax.

Real Estate Taxi inference: Canada has already experienced a meaningful real-price correction from the pandemic-era peak even where nominal prices remain visually high. This improves valuation entry points but does not automatically restore affordability because mortgage payments and household income are separate constraints.

06

6. Houses versus apartments and condominium units

Property-type performance has separated sharply in several markets. Ontario’s June benchmark data showed apartments down 8.0% year over year, townhouses down 6.6%, and single-family homes down 4.2%. Ottawa showed a similar hierarchy in May: apartment benchmarks fell 6.7%, while single-family benchmarks increased 0.3%.

This reflects more than buyer taste. Investor-owned condominiums are sensitive to mortgage carry, condo fees, rent regulation, new-completion competition and investor resale. Ground-oriented housing often has scarcer land content and stronger family demand, but it also carries higher absolute prices, maintenance costs and insurance exposure.

Due-diligence implication: a discounted condo is not automatically cheap. Review reserve-fund studies, special assessments, insurance deductibles, litigation, short-term-rental rules, owner-occupancy ratios, building systems and the pipeline of competing completions.
07

7. New-build versus existing-home pricing

Statistics Canada’s New Housing Price Index declined 0.3% month over month in May 2026. The national total index moved from 121.5 in January to 120.7 in May (December 2016 = 100). This measures agreed selling prices for new houses while holding specifications constant; it does not cover all condominium presales or every resale segment.

Residential building construction costs across a 15-CMA composite increased 0.6% in the first quarter of 2026 and 2.8% year over year. Therefore, developers may face a squeeze between softer buyer demand and still-rising input costs. Incentives, upgrades, deposit structures and assignment terms can become as important as headline list-price discounts.

Compare the effective price after incentives, GST/HST treatment, parking and storage, development charges embedded in price, occupancy fees, closing adjustments and anticipated condo fees—not only the advertised base price.
08

8. Transaction volume, liquidity and time-to-exit

CREA’s July forecast expects 463,336 residential transactions in 2026, 1.4% fewer than in 2025, followed by a 3.7% increase to 480,567 in 2027. The forecast assumes slow positive growth, stable interest rates and stable or modestly rising prices, but it is not a guarantee.

National liquidity improved from March through June, yet local exit risk remains important. A property can be located in a balanced national market and still be illiquid because it is luxury-priced, rural, remote, highly customized, tenanted, legally complex or in a building with known defects.

  • Use local months of inventory by property type and price band.
  • Measure days on market and sale-to-list ratio for comparable completed transactions.
  • Stress-test a 6–12 month resale period, not an immediate exit.
  • For luxury and rural assets, require a wider valuation range and larger liquidity reserve.

10

10. Where supply is improving—and where it is vulnerable

CMHC reported that 2025 housing starts increased 6% to about 259,000 units, driven by record rental construction and more “missing middle” housing. Completions added supply in Vancouver, Calgary and Edmonton. However, ownership-oriented construction weakened, condominium presales collapsed in key markets, unsold inventory rose and financing conditions tightened.

Toronto was a notable weakness: starts fell below its historical average and were the lowest per capita among the seven large CMAs reviewed by CMHC. Calgary surpassed Toronto in starts in 2025, while Edmonton reached a second consecutive record and expanded both rental and ownership supply.

Real Estate Taxi inference: the near-term buyer may benefit from completed inventory and developer incentives, but reduced presales and cancelled projects can create a later supply gap. The effect differs by tenure: purpose-built rental supply can expand even while ownership condominium supply contracts.
11

11. Vacancy, underused stock and conversion risk

Canada does not have one reliable national “empty homes” measure that can be used as a current investment statistic across all municipalities. Vacancy taxes and declarations are local or provincial, and exemptions differ. Investors should avoid treating a visible inventory increase as proof of permanent oversupply.

For conversion or redevelopment, verify:

  • municipal zoning and permitted density;
  • development charges, parkland and servicing requirements;
  • heritage designation and environmental constraints;
  • tenant relocation, renoviction and replacement-rental rules;
  • building code, accessibility and fire-safety upgrades;
  • financing conditions and presale requirements.

Land value can be impaired by approvals risk even when the surrounding market is strong.

RENTAL INTELLIGENCE

Long-Term Rents, Vacancy, Yields & Short-Term Rental Risk

National rental pressure eased in 2025–2026, but affordable units remain tight and local rules can materially change returns.

12

12. Long-term rental market: conditions eased but affordability remains difficult

CMHC’s 2025 survey of major centres found that the purpose-built apartment vacancy rate increased to 3.1% from 2.2% in 2024, moving above the 10-year average. The average two-bedroom rent paid by all tenants was CAD 1,550, up 5.1%. The surveyed condominium rental vacancy rate was lower at 1.3%, while the average two-bedroom condominium rent was CAD 2,305.

Higher completions, slower population growth and weaker household formation reduced rent pressure. Turnover rents declined in Vancouver, Calgary, Toronto and Halifax, and some landlords offered incentives such as free rent, moving allowances or signing bonuses. Affordable units remained the most competitive segment.

Do not project in-place rent growth from asking-rent headlines. Under rent regulation, existing-tenant rent, lawful annual increases, turnover rent and market asking rent can follow different paths.
13

13. Gross rental-yield estimates versus realistic net yield

Global Property Guide estimated an average 5.72% gross rental yield for selected Canadian cities in Q1 2026 using advertised purchase prices and advertised rents. Its city averages included approximately 4.24% in Montréal, 6.27% in Toronto, 5.75% in Vancouver, 5.99% in Ottawa and 6.87% in Calgary.

These are portal-based gross estimates, not official transaction-based investor returns. They can be useful for screening but may be distorted by property mix, listing quality, furnished units and mismatched purchase/rent samples. The same source notes that net yields are often 1.5–2 percentage points lower.

  • Deduct municipal property tax and any vacancy tax.
  • Deduct condo/strata fees and special assessments.
  • Model insurance, repairs, appliances, utilities and snow/landscaping.
  • Include leasing commissions, management and legal/accounting costs.
  • Stress vacancy, bad debt and rent-control limits.
  • Calculate after-tax cash flow, not only capitalization rate.

15

15. Short-term rentals: regulation is property-specific

Short-term-rental legality and economics vary by province, municipality and condominium corporation. Several major cities limit rentals to a principal residence, require licences or impose platform and reporting obligations. Federal tax policy also denies certain income-tax deductions for non-compliant short-term rentals.

Before underwriting nightly income, verify the exact address against:

  • municipal zoning and licensing;
  • principal-residence requirements;
  • condominium bylaws and declarations;
  • provincial registration or tourism rules;
  • fire, occupancy and safety standards;
  • sales-tax, accommodation-tax and income-tax obligations;
  • insurance coverage for commercial guest use.
Investment rule: value the property first on a legal long-term-rental or owner-use basis. Treat short-term-rental income as an upside case only after written regulatory confirmation.
FINANCING & AFFORDABILITY

Mortgage Conditions, Qualification & Buyer Capacity

Lower policy rates have improved sentiment, but fixed mortgage pricing, renewal shocks and income qualification remain decisive.

16

16. Bank of Canada rate and mortgage transmission

On July 15, 2026, the Bank of Canada held the overnight rate at 2.25%, the Bank Rate at 2.5% and the deposit rate at 2.20%. The Bank said economic activity was improving but highlighted risks from the Middle East conflict and United States trade policy.

The policy rate is not a consumer mortgage quote. Variable-rate products tend to respond to prime rates, while fixed mortgage rates depend heavily on Government of Canada bond yields and lender spreads. Borrower credit, loan-to-value ratio, insured status, occupancy type and property type also affect pricing.

Renewal risk remains important. Bank of Canada analysis published in 2025 estimated that about 60% of mortgage holders renewing in 2025–2026 would face payment increases, with the average 2026 renewal payment potentially around 6% above December 2024 payments for affected borrowers.

17

17. Down payment, mortgage insurance and qualification

For insured owner-occupied purchases below CAD 1.5 million, CMHC guidance states the minimum down payment is 5% on the first CAD 500,000 and 10% on the portion above CAD 500,000. Homes priced at CAD 1.5 million or more require at least 20% down and are not eligible for CMHC homeowner mortgage insurance.

Insurance availability is not loan approval. Lenders assess income, debt service, credit, property marketability and the applicable mortgage stress test. Investment properties, non-residents, self-employed borrowers and unusual properties may require higher equity and stronger documentation.

  • Obtain financing approval before waiving conditions.
  • Confirm the lender will finance the specific building and property type.
  • Stress-test renewal at a higher rate.
  • Keep liquidity for closing adjustments and repairs.
  • Do not use the maximum approved loan as the investment budget.
18

18. Affordability: national averages understate household stress

Statistics Canada’s latest broad affordability study reported that 22.0% of households spent at least 30% of pre-tax income on shelter costs in 2022; renters were much more likely to cross that threshold than owners. These data are not current enough to describe mid-2026 conditions, but they show the structural affordability problem.

Affordability improved where prices corrected and rates declined, yet the improvement is uneven. Toronto and Vancouver remain high-price markets relative to income; many first-time buyers depend on family support or smaller units. Lower-priced provinces can be more affordable, but job concentration, population flows, property liquidity and rent depth must be tested.

The best “affordability” market is not necessarily the lowest-priced market. Sustainable affordability combines purchase price, local income, employment resilience, insurance, taxes, transport costs and resale demand.
TAX & LEGAL

Foreign-Buyer Rules, Purchase Costs, Ownership Taxes & Buying Procedure

Canada’s tax and ownership rules are federal, provincial and municipal. The exact address and buyer status determine the answer.

19

19. Federal foreign-buyer restriction in 2026

The federal government extended the prohibition on purchases of covered residential property by non-Canadians to January 1, 2027. The prohibition applies through statutory definitions of non-Canadian buyers, residential property and geographic coverage, with exemptions and exceptions.

This is not a simple “all foreigners can never buy” rule, but it is also not safe to rely on an informal exception. A buyer should obtain Canadian legal advice before signing, especially where the buyer is a temporary resident, spouse of an eligible buyer, corporation, trust, developer, purchaser of vacant land, or buyer outside a defined census area.

Do not use residency-by-investment assumptions. Canada does not offer a general federal residence permit simply because a person buys a home. Immigration eligibility and property ownership are separate legal questions.

21

21. General land-transfer and purchase costs

Purchase costs vary significantly. Common items include provincial land transfer or property transfer tax, Toronto’s separate municipal land transfer tax, legal fees, title insurance, inspection, appraisal, survey, lender costs and closing adjustments. New or substantially renovated homes can involve GST/HST, with rebates depending on buyer eligibility and use.

Examples illustrate the regional range. British Columbia’s general property transfer tax is 1% up to CAD 200,000, 2% from CAD 200,000 to CAD 2 million, and 3% above CAD 2 million, with an additional 2% on the residential portion above CAD 3 million. Toronto revised high-value MLTT rates from April 1, 2026, reaching higher marginal rates for expensive one- and two-family residential property.

Obtain a written closing-cost statement before making an unconditional offer. Foreign-buyer taxes can be larger than the down payment assumptions used in generic online calculators.
22

22. Annual ownership taxes and vacant-home exposure

Municipal property tax is the recurring baseline and depends on local assessed value and tax rates. Some jurisdictions add vacancy or speculation taxes. In British Columbia designated areas, the 2026 speculation and vacancy tax rate is 3% for foreign owners and untaxed worldwide earners and 1% for Canadian citizens or permanent residents who are not untaxed worldwide earners, subject to exemptions.

The federal Underused Housing Tax was eliminated for 2025 and later tax years, although obligations and penalties for 2022–2024 remain relevant. This is an example of why stale tax summaries can mislead buyers.

  • Check municipal vacant-home declarations.
  • Check provincial speculation/vacancy tax coverage.
  • Confirm principal-residence or tenancy exemptions.
  • Model tax on assessed value, not only purchase price.
  • Keep documentary proof of occupancy and qualifying rentals.
23

23. Rental income, non-resident withholding and capital gains

Canadian rental income and property dispositions have tax consequences that depend on residence, ownership structure, use, depreciation claims and treaty position. Non-resident owners can face withholding and filing procedures on rent. On sale, non-resident compliance certificates and purchaser withholding mechanisms may apply. Capital gains rules are separate from the principal-residence exemption available to qualifying residents.

This report does not state one national effective tax rate because that would be misleading. Federal and provincial income tax, deductible expenses, capital cost allowance, recapture, ownership through a corporation or trust, and changes to capital-gains inclusion rules can alter the result.

Before purchase, obtain a cross-border tax memo covering annual rental filings, withholding, indirect taxes, estate planning, beneficial ownership reporting and sale procedures. Structure should be decided before title is taken, not after.
24

24. Standard buying procedure and protective conditions

  1. Confirm the buyer is legally eligible to acquire the property and obtain tax advice.
  2. Engage a local real estate lawyer/notary as appropriate for the province.
  3. Review title, survey, easements, restrictive covenants, taxes, utilities and zoning.
  4. Submit an offer with financing, inspection, insurance, document-review and legal conditions where appropriate.
  5. For condominiums, review status/estoppel documents, reserve fund, budgets, bylaws, minutes, insurance and litigation.
  6. Complete inspection and specialist reports; negotiate repairs or price.
  7. Finalize mortgage, appraisal, insurance and source-of-funds documentation.
  8. Lawyer completes searches, statement of adjustments, transfer documents and registration.
  9. Conduct final walk-through and verify vacant possession or tenancy terms.
  10. Retain complete closing and tax records.
Electronic registration systems are efficient, but they do not replace physical-condition, zoning, condominium and environmental due diligence.
25

25. Title, zoning, permit and property-condition risks

  • Title: liens, easements, restrictive covenants, boundary issues, Indigenous rights context and unregistered interests.
  • Zoning: legal use, secondary suites, density, parking, short-term rentals and future nearby development.
  • Permits: additions, basement units, decks, pools, electrical, plumbing and occupancy approvals.
  • Environmental: contamination, buried oil tanks, floodplain, wetlands, septic systems and wells.
  • Condition: foundation, water intrusion, roof, envelope, mould, asbestos, radon, electrical and heating systems.
  • Tenancy: lawful rent, deposits, arrears, notices, eviction restrictions and vacant-possession risk.

Seller representations are useful but should be verified through documents, inspections and professional advice.

26

26. Condominium / strata due diligence

  • Latest financial statements, operating budget and reserve-fund study.
  • Special assessments approved, discussed or reasonably foreseeable.
  • Insurance policy, deductibles and claims history.
  • Building-envelope, balcony, elevator, plumbing and garage issues.
  • Board minutes and owner correspondence.
  • Rental, pet, renovation and short-term-rental restrictions.
  • Developer control, warranty status and unsold-unit concentration.
  • Litigation and construction-defect claims.
  • Parking, locker and limited-common-property rights.
  • Actual monthly fee plus utilities not included.
Low condo fees can indicate underfunding rather than efficiency. Compare the reserve fund with the building’s age and major-component replacement schedule.
27

27. Off-plan and new-development risk

Canadian pre-construction contracts can allocate significant risk to the buyer. Review deposit protection, rescission rights, material-change clauses, occupancy timing, outside completion dates, assignment restrictions, development-charge caps, interim occupancy fees, area tolerances and the developer’s cancellation rights.

In markets with weak presales and rising unsold inventory, buyers should also test:

  • developer financial capacity and track record;
  • warranty coverage and claim history;
  • financing-appraisal risk at completion;
  • whether the lender will recognize assignments or investor use;
  • closing tax treatment and rebate eligibility;
  • competition from discounted unsold developer inventory.
A long completion period creates a future financing obligation, not a fixed-return investment. The buyer may have to close even if market value or mortgage capacity declines.
RISK & STRATEGY

Climate, Insurance, Investor Fit & 2026–2028 Scenarios

The most attractive Canadian market depends on the investor’s objective, risk tolerance and ability to operate locally.

28

28. Climate, insurance and natural-hazard risk

Canada’s hazard profile is regional: river and coastal flood, wildfire, smoke, hail, severe winter, freeze-thaw, wind, earthquake exposure in British Columbia, permafrost and remote-service constraints. Insurance availability, deductibles and exclusions can change rapidly after major loss events.

  • Obtain an insurability quote before waiving conditions.
  • Review flood maps and sewer-backup exposure.
  • Check wildfire interface and evacuation access.
  • Confirm earthquake coverage and deductibles where relevant.
  • Inspect roof, drainage, sump pumps, grading and foundation.
  • Budget for snow, heating, freeze protection and seasonal vacancy.
  • For waterfront property, review erosion, shoreline regulation and septic risk.
A property is not financeable at the expected terms if adequate insurance cannot be obtained. Insurance should be treated as a condition of purchase, not a post-closing administrative step.

30. Opportunity map

  • Negotiated completed inventory: developer or resale units where carrying costs and competing supply create motivated sellers.
  • Family-oriented ground housing: scarce, functional homes in employment-connected, supply-constrained areas.
  • Legal small multi-unit properties: where rents, zoning and financing support diversified income.
  • Ottawa-style segment divergence: selective value in apartments only after building and supply analysis.
  • Prairie markets: better rent-to-price relationships in some cities, balanced against construction growth and economic cyclicality.
  • Atlantic markets: lower entry prices and recent appreciation, balanced against smaller liquidity pools and non-resident taxes.

The opportunity is primarily in selection and negotiation, not in buying the national index.

  • Reserve fund and special assessments
  • Developer track record and warranty
  • Assignment and occupancy clauses
  • Mortgage approval for the exact property
  • Renewal and rate stress test
  • Net rental model with capital reserve
  • Exit period and selling-cost stress test
  • Final walk-through and vacant possession
  • Post-closing compliance calendar
34

34. Negotiation intelligence for 2026 buyers

Negotiating power is strongest where active inventory is high, investor demand is weak, the property has been relisted, developer inventory competes with resale, or the building faces upcoming capital work. The best negotiation is often not a simple price cut.

  • Request a longer financing or document-review condition.
  • Negotiate repair credits or holdbacks.
  • Cap development charges and closing adjustments in pre-construction deals.
  • Seek parking, storage, upgrades, fee credits or mortgage-rate buydowns.
  • Require delivery of vacant possession where legally possible.
  • Align closing with lease, tax and financing strategy.
In a balanced market, accurate underwriting has more value than aggressive bidding tactics. Walk away where legal, insurance or building risks cannot be quantified.
35

35. Final investment conclusion

Canada in mid-2026 is a selective market, not a uniform recovery trade. The national MLS HPI has stopped declining month over month, but remains below last year. More inventory and softer investor-oriented segments create opportunities in parts of Ontario and British Columbia, while several lower-priced provinces continue to show stronger annual price growth.

The strongest acquisition cases combine: a legal and financeable buyer, a property with durable local demand, conservative leverage, verified insurance, a realistic net-income model, clean title/building documentation and an exit plan that does not depend on rapid national appreciation.

The weakest cases rely on gross portal yields, short-term-rental income without legal confirmation, maximum leverage, stale tax rules, or the assumption that all Canadian markets will follow Toronto and Vancouver in the same direction.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Canada a buyer’s or seller’s market in mid-2026?
Nationally, the June 2026 sales-to-new-listings ratio was 50.2% and months of inventory were 4.8, both consistent with a broadly balanced market. Local conditions differ materially: parts of Ontario and British Columbia offer buyers more selection, while several lower-priced provinces remain tighter.
Are Canadian home prices rising or falling in 2026?
The national MLS Home Price Index was unchanged from May to June 2026 but remained 3.6% below June 2025. The national average sale price was 0.5% higher year over year because average prices are influenced by the mix and location of homes sold.
Can a foreign buyer purchase residential property in Canada in 2026?
Many non-Canadians are prohibited from purchasing covered residential property under the federal ban extended to January 1, 2027, subject to statutory definitions and exemptions. Provincial and municipal foreign-buyer taxes may also apply. Legal advice is essential before signing.
What gross rental yield can an investor expect in Canada?
A Global Property Guide asking-price and asking-rent sample estimated a 5.72% average gross yield in Q1 2026 across selected cities. This is not an official transaction-based measure. Realistic net yield can be materially lower after property tax, condo fees, insurance, maintenance, management, vacancy and income tax.
What is the Bank of Canada policy rate?
On July 15, 2026, the Bank of Canada held the overnight rate at 2.25%. Mortgage pricing still depends on lender spreads, borrower qualification, term, insured status and bond-market conditions.
How much inventory is available nationally?
At the end of June 2026, CREA reported 208,578 residential properties listed on Canadian MLS systems and 4.8 months of inventory nationally.
Are rents still rising?
CMHC reported that the 2025 average purpose-built two-bedroom rent across surveyed major markets was CAD 1,550, up 5.1%, while the vacancy rate rose to 3.1%. Turnover rents weakened in several major cities, indicating that asking rent and in-place rent trends can diverge.
What is the biggest due-diligence mistake in Canada?
Treating Canada as one uniform legal and investment market. Taxes, rent regulation, zoning, short-term-rental rules, title practices, insurance exposure and foreign-buyer costs vary by province and municipality, while property-level condition and condominium documents can change the economics of a deal.
