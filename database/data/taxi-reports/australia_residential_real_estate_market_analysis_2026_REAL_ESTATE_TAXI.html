DIRECT ANALYSIS ANSWER

Australia remains structurally undersupplied, but 2026 is a financing-sensitive and increasingly segmented market.

The central investment tension is simple: population-driven demand and insufficient housing delivery support values and rents, yet the cost of debt is high enough to punish weak cash flow, overpayment and low-quality assets. Western Australia and Queensland have shown stronger momentum than Victoria, while Sydney retains scarcity and global depth but carries the highest affordability burden. For a foreign buyer, the market is currently not a normal “choose any resale home” market: most established dwellings are generally prohibited until 30 June 2029, subject to limited exceptions, so new dwellings, qualifying redevelopment and supply-adding projects are the relevant channels.

A serious buyer should therefore select the legal eligibility route first, the state and property type second, and the individual asset third. Yield estimates must be converted from headline gross percentages into a full net model including vacancy, management, insurance, repairs, rates, land tax, strata levies and financing.

AUSTRALIA MARKET INTELLIGENCE

Data separation: what each number actually means

Australia has several widely quoted price series that answer different questions. This section prevents official stock-value data, private valuation models, asking prices and transaction evidence from being mixed together.

01

Official data, private estimates and Real Estate Taxi inference

Official transaction and stock data: the Australian Bureau of Statistics publishes dwelling stock values, mean dwelling prices, building approvals, construction activity, lending commitments, CPI rents and macroeconomic data. These are the strongest sources for national and state-level measurement, but they are not always suburb-level and the mean dwelling price is not the same as a median sale price.

Private market estimates: Cotality and other analytics providers publish frequently updated modelled dwelling values, median values, rents and vacancy estimates. These are useful for timely market direction but remain vendor methodologies rather than government transaction registers.

Asking-price data: portal listings describe seller expectations, not completed prices. Asking prices can rise because the listing mix becomes more expensive even when comparable properties are not appreciating.

Real Estate Taxi analysis: conclusions in this report combine the source data, legal structure and investment logic. They are analytical judgments, not valuations, legal advice, tax advice or return guarantees.

Never compare an ABS mean dwelling price directly with a portal median asking price and call the difference “undervaluation.” They measure different populations and use different methodologies.
02

Executive market conclusion

Australia’s national housing story is still dominated by a shortage of completed dwellings relative to household formation and population needs. The shortage is strongest where planning, infrastructure, construction costs and apartment feasibility constrain delivery.

  • Price support: scarce stock, population growth and limited new supply.
  • Price restraint: RBA cash rate of 4.35%, new mortgage rates above 6% and severe affordability pressure.
  • Best relative momentum: Queensland and Western Australia in official March 2026 stock-value data and private early-2026 market estimates.
  • Best liquidity depth: Sydney and Melbourne, but at lower current yields and higher sensitivity to borrowing capacity.
  • Foreign-buyer reality: new dwellings and approved supply-adding investments, not ordinary established homes, are the main legal pathways.

The correct investment question is not “Will Australian property rise?” but “Which state, tenure, buyer class, supply pipeline and financing structure can still produce an acceptable risk-adjusted net return?”

04

Nominal versus inflation-adjusted performance

Global Property Guide’s March 2026 summary reported a 2.41% year-on-year nominal house-price change for Q1 2026 and a -1.63% real change after inflation adjustment. The distinction matters: an asset can rise in Australian dollars yet lose purchasing power after inflation.

ABS monthly CPI data later showed annual inflation pressures remained elevated in May 2026, with new dwelling prices up 5.6% and rents up 3.6%. This means investors should calculate real total return after acquisition costs, annual ownership costs, tax and financing—not merely quote nominal capital growth.

For highly leveraged investors, the relevant hurdle is not CPI alone. The property’s net operating yield and expected price growth must exceed the effective mortgage cost and transaction-cost drag over the intended holding period.

07

Houses versus apartments

Detached houses generally carry a larger land component and therefore benefit more directly from land scarcity, but entry prices, maintenance and insurance can be higher. Apartments can offer lower entry prices and stronger gross yields, yet the result depends heavily on building quality, owners-corporation governance, special levies and future competing supply.

In 2026, detached approvals were more resilient than higher-density approvals. ABS May data showed private-sector house approvals rose 2.8% to 10,537, while private dwellings excluding houses fell 10.4% to 6,034. This does not mean every apartment market is oversupplied; it shows the apartment pipeline remains volatile and project feasibility is difficult.

  • House investor: prioritize land, transport, school catchments, flood/fire exposure and realistic maintenance.
  • Apartment investor: prioritize strata records, defects, insurance, sinking funds, cladding/fire compliance, owner concentration and nearby pipeline.
08

New-build versus established homes

For domestic buyers, established homes dominate transaction and lending volumes because they provide immediate use and established neighbourhood evidence. For foreign buyers in 2026, however, the legal framework changes the opportunity set: established dwellings are generally unavailable, while new or near-new dwellings and qualifying development can be permitted.

New builds may carry builder, completion, settlement-valuation and defect risk. Established dwellings may carry hidden condition, renovation, termite, asbestos, unapproved work and energy-efficiency risk. The correct comparison is total cost to achieve a compliant, lettable, insurable property—not the headline price alone.

A foreign buyer should never pay a “new-build premium” without testing whether the premium is justified by scarcity, quality, depreciation benefits, rental demand and resale depth among domestic buyers.

Supply, construction feasibility and underlying demand

Approvals improved from a low base, but completions remain insufficient and the high-density pipeline is the weak point.

10

Current approvals: pipeline improving, but not yet solved

ABS May 2026 data recorded 17,019 seasonally adjusted dwelling approvals, down 1.1% from April but 5.3% above May 2025. Private-sector house approvals reached 10,537, the highest level since September 2021, while non-house approvals fell sharply during the month.

Approvals are a leading indicator, not completed supply. Projects can be delayed, redesigned or abandoned because of finance, labour, materials, presales, infrastructure or builder failure. Investors should examine commencements and completions as well as approvals.

The strongest price support comes where demand is rising and completed supply—not merely approved supply—remains difficult to deliver.

12

Why apartment supply remains difficult

Apartment construction requires a viable relationship between land cost, build cost, finance, presales and end values. When interest rates and construction costs are high, a project can be approved yet financially unbuildable. This is particularly relevant in Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane where higher-density housing is essential to supply targets.

  • Longer planning and construction timelines increase cost uncertainty.
  • Presale buyers face settlement-valuation risk if bank valuations are below contract price.
  • Builder insolvency and defect remediation can transfer costs to owners.
  • Large simultaneous completions can temporarily weaken rents and resale liquidity.
13

Population, migration and household demand

ABS data showed net overseas migration of 306,000 people in 2024–25, down from 429,000 a year earlier. The decline reduces one source of demand acceleration, but the level remains significant and does not eliminate the existing housing shortage.

Housing demand depends on more than population: household size, student numbers, internal migration, job growth, affordability, remote work and the availability of rental stock all matter. Queensland and Western Australia have benefited from interstate and employment-driven demand, while inner-city apartment demand is more exposed to students and temporary migrants.

Australia’s GDP grew 0.3% in the March quarter 2026 and 2.5% over the year, providing a positive but not booming macro backdrop.

15

Gross yield versus realistic net yield

Global Property Guide estimated Australia’s average gross residential rental yield at 4.69% in February 2026, with Darwin the highest among reviewed capitals at 6.14%. Gross yield is annual rent divided by purchase price before expenses.

A realistic net model should deduct:

  • property management and letting fees;
  • vacancy and bad-debt allowance;
  • repairs, maintenance and capital replacements;
  • council and water charges;
  • building, landlord and catastrophe insurance;
  • strata levies and special levies;
  • land tax and foreign-owner surcharges where applicable;
  • accounting, compliance and tax;
  • finance cost.
A 4.7% gross yield can easily become a low single-digit net yield before interest. Highly leveraged purchases at 6%+ debt cost may be cash-flow negative even in a tight rental market.
16

Short-term rental opportunity and regulatory fragmentation

Australia has strong domestic and international tourism, but short-term rental economics are highly local. State registration rules, local planning controls, strata by-laws, fire-safety duties, caps on hosted or unhosted nights and building insurance can all affect legal operation.

Underwrite short-term rentals using a conservative annual occupancy model, not peak-event nightly rates. Include platform fees, cleaning, linen, utilities, furnishing replacement, guest damage, local management and seasonality. Compare the result with a long-term lease after all costs and regulatory risk.

There is no single national Airbnb rule. Verify the exact state, council and strata regime before purchase.

17

Tenancy regulation and landlord execution risk

Residential tenancy law is state and territory based. Notice periods, rent-increase rules, minimum standards, pet rules, bond handling, termination grounds and dispute procedures differ. Investors must model compliance and time-to-recover-possession, not assume a uniform national landlord regime.

  • Review current legislation and regulator guidance for the target state.
  • Check mandatory safety, smoke alarm and energy requirements.
  • Use a licensed property manager with local arrears and tribunal experience.
  • Do not base return assumptions on illegal rent increases or unrealistic vacancy-free operation.

19

Lending activity and buyer sensitivity

ABS Lending Indicators for the March quarter 2026 showed the number of new dwelling loan commitments fell 6.2% quarter-on-quarter and the value fell 3.8%. Owner-occupier, investor and first-home-buyer commitments all declined.

This is evidence that higher rates are affecting effective demand even while structural housing need remains strong. It supports a segmented outlook: scarce lower-priced stock can remain competitive while higher-value discretionary segments become more negotiable.

A buyer should stress-test repayments at a rate above the current offer and include a vacancy period. The asset must survive the financing scenario, not only the optimistic price scenario.
20

Affordability and deposit risk

Affordability is one of Australia’s largest market constraints. High prices, stamp duty, required deposits and mortgage serviceability reduce the number of qualified buyers. This can cap price growth in Sydney and Melbourne even when listings are limited.

For investors, affordability risk appears in two places: tenant capacity to absorb rent increases and resale-buyer capacity to finance the future purchase. Assets priced above local income support need stronger scarcity, wealth-buyer depth or rental fundamentals.

  • Calculate total cash required, not just deposit.
  • Include stamp duty, surcharge, legal, inspections and loan costs.
  • Do not assume future refinancing at a lower rate.
  • Test rent at market evidence, not a developer guarantee.
21

Non-resident finance and currency risk

A foreign buyer may face Australian lender restrictions, higher equity requirements, verification of overseas income and currency translation risk. If income is earned in another currency but debt and property expenses are in Australian dollars, exchange-rate movements can raise the real cost of servicing the property.

Currency gains can also amplify returns when converting sale proceeds home, but that is not predictable. The investment model should separate property return from foreign-exchange speculation.

Obtain lender pre-assessment before signing. FIRB eligibility does not guarantee finance, and finance approval does not guarantee FIRB eligibility.

Foreign buyers, taxes, purchase procedure and ownership risk

Australia combines federal foreign-investment rules with state-level duties, land tax, tenancy law and conveyancing practice.

22

Foreign-buyer rules in force in July 2026

Official Australian Government guidance updated 1 July 2026 states that foreign investors generally need to notify the Australian Taxation Office before acquiring residential land, regardless of value. Policy channels foreign investment toward new housing and supply creation.

  • Established dwellings: generally prohibited from 1 April 2025 to 30 June 2029, subject to limited exceptions.
  • New or near-new dwellings: may generally be acquired with required approval/notification.
  • Vacant residential land: approval typically includes a condition to complete construction within four years.
  • Registration: acquisitions and disposals must be reported to the Register of Foreign Ownership of Australian Assets.
  • Vacancy fee: relevant foreign-owned homes can attract an annual vacancy fee if not occupied or genuinely available for rent for more than 183 days in a vacancy year.
Do not rely on a sales agent’s verbal statement that a property is “FIRB approved.” Obtain written legal advice on your status, the asset classification, approval conditions, fees and settlement timing.

24

Purchase costs and annual ownership costs

Acquisition costs can include: transfer duty, foreign purchaser surcharge, FIRB/ATO application fee, conveyancing or solicitor fees, title and government searches, building and pest inspection, strata report, loan valuation, mortgage registration and lender fees. Buyer’s agent fees may also apply.

Annual costs can include: council rates, water charges, land tax, foreign-owner surcharge, insurance, strata levies, property management, maintenance, safety compliance, vacancy and accounting. Short-term rentals add furnishing, utilities, cleaning and platform costs.

Stamp duty is a large holding-period penalty. A short-term resale may require substantial price growth merely to recover entry and exit costs.

25

Rental income, deductions and tax

Australian rental income must generally be declared. ATO guidance allows eligible deductions where a property is rented or genuinely available for rent on commercial terms, subject to apportionment and specific rules. Common categories include interest, agent fees, repairs, council rates, insurance and certain depreciation or capital works deductions.

Repairs and capital improvements are not treated the same. Private use, below-market rent and holiday-home use can restrict deductions. Foreign owners must also consider Australian tax residency, withholding and treaty interaction.

Tax deductions do not turn a poor investment into a good one. Negative gearing reduces taxable income but does not eliminate the cash loss.
26

Capital gains tax and foreign-resident withholding

Capital gains tax treatment depends on ownership structure, tax residency, dates, use and available exemptions. Foreign residents generally cannot claim the main-residence exemption for property sold after 30 June 2020 unless a limited life-events test applies, and their CGT discount entitlement can be restricted.

Australia’s foreign-resident capital-gains-withholding regime can require the purchaser to withhold from the sale proceeds unless the vendor provides the required clearance or variation. The current ATO guidance should be checked immediately before settlement because rates and thresholds have changed over time.

Obtain tax advice before acquisition, not only before sale. Ownership through a company, trust, partnership or individual can materially change tax, finance and estate-planning outcomes.

27

Standard buying procedure

  1. Confirm buyer status, foreign-investment eligibility and finance.
  2. Select a state, property type and legal tenure.
  3. Appoint an independent solicitor or licensed conveyancer before signing.
  4. Review contract, title, planning, zoning, easements, restrictions and disclosures.
  5. Order building/pest inspection and, for apartments, a strata/owners-corporation records review.
  6. Confirm duty, surcharge, annual taxes and insurance availability.
  7. Negotiate conditions for finance, due diligence and FIRB/ATO approval where permitted.
  8. Exchange contracts and pay the deposit; cooling-off rules vary and auctions often have none.
  9. Complete final inspection, settlement statement and transfer registration.
  10. Register foreign ownership and comply with vacancy and tax reporting.

The procedure varies by state. In auction purchases, the contract is generally unconditional immediately and there is no cooling-off period.

28

Title, easements, strata and planning risk

Australia largely uses registered title systems, but a registered title does not eliminate property-specific risk. Review:

  • ownership and encumbrances;
  • mortgages, covenants, easements and rights of way;
  • survey boundaries and encroachments;
  • zoning, overlays, heritage and development controls;
  • building approvals and occupancy certificates;
  • unapproved renovations or secondary dwellings;
  • strata by-laws, minutes, insurance, defects, litigation and sinking funds;
  • special levies and cladding or fire-safety works.

A low-priced apartment with a weak owners corporation can become expensive through special levies and resale stigma.

29

Off-plan and new-development risk

Off-plan buyers commit before the finished asset can be inspected. Material risks include delayed completion, plan changes, smaller usable area, inferior finishes, sunset clauses, developer insolvency, bank valuation shortfall at settlement, defects and high initial strata costs.

NSW, for example, provides a 10-business-day cooling-off period for off-plan contracts, compared with the usual five business days for established private-treaty purchases, but state rules differ.

  • Investigate developer and builder history.
  • Review sunset, variation and rescission clauses.
  • Check deposit protection and stakeholder arrangements.
  • Model a lower bank valuation at settlement.
  • Inspect comparable completed projects, not only display suites.
30

Climate, natural hazard and insurance risk

Australian property risk is strongly location-specific. Flood, bushfire, cyclone, coastal erosion, hail, extreme heat, subsidence and storm-surge exposure can affect insurance cost, finance, maintenance, rental interruptions and resale demand.

Insurance should be quoted before the contract becomes unconditional. A property can be legally buildable yet economically unattractive if annual premiums, excesses or exclusions are severe.

  • Check government hazard maps and local planning overlays.
  • Review past claims and flood certificates where available.
  • Inspect drainage, roof, cladding, retaining walls and termite risk.
  • For strata, review the building policy, exclusions and claims history.

Investor fit, opportunities, risks and 2026–2028 scenarios

The strongest strategy depends on buyer eligibility, debt cost, holding period and ability to perform deep property-level due diligence.

31

Who the market may suit

  • Long-horizon domestic buyers: seeking scarce land or well-located housing and able to absorb rate volatility.
  • Income investors: focusing on lower entry prices, tight vacancy and low-maintenance assets with a credible net yield.
  • Foreign buyers: legally eligible for new dwellings or supply-adding projects and willing to manage approval, surcharge and vacancy obligations.
  • Value-add buyers: able to execute compliant renovation or redevelopment in supply-constrained locations.
  • Capital-preservation buyers: prioritizing prime, liquid locations over maximum yield.

The market may be unsuitable for highly leveraged short-term speculators, buyers relying on guaranteed rent, or investors who cannot tolerate negative cash flow and large transaction costs.

32

Opportunity map

Perth / Western Australia: strong recent momentum and housing demand, but rapid appreciation requires disciplined purchase pricing and attention to commodity-cycle and supply response.

Brisbane / South-East Queensland: population growth, infrastructure and scarcity support demand; flood, insurance and apartment-pipeline due diligence are essential.

Adelaide: relative affordability and tight supply support demand, but smaller market depth requires suburb-level liquidity checks.

Melbourne: weaker recent price performance and higher apartment supply can create selective value, but state taxes, building quality and rental regulation must be included.

Sydney: deepest prestige and employment market, but high entry cost and low yields demand a long holding period and exceptional asset selection.

34

Approximately two-year scenario framework

Base scenario: nominal prices continue to rise unevenly because completed supply remains short, but higher mortgage rates keep Sydney and Melbourne slower than selected mid-sized capitals. Rent growth moderates but remains positive.

Upside scenario: inflation eases faster, borrowing costs decline, construction remains constrained and buyer capacity improves. Quality assets in supply-limited areas reaccelerate.

Downside scenario: inflation or external shocks keep rates high, unemployment rises, investor lending weakens and affordability forces more listings. Highly leveraged, low-yield and recently overbuilt submarkets underperform.

The scenarios are not price forecasts. They are decision frameworks. A sound property should remain acceptable under the downside financing and vacancy assumptions.

36

Real Estate Taxi final verdict

Australia in July 2026 is not a simple high-yield market and not a uniform national boom. It is a supply-constrained, high-cost, legally fragmented market in which the strongest recent growth has shifted toward Queensland, Western Australia and selected smaller capitals.

The highest-quality opportunities are likely to be assets with broad domestic resale demand, limited competing supply, insurable construction, manageable annual costs and a net income profile that does not depend on immediate rate cuts. For foreign buyers, legal eligibility and state-level surcharge analysis are part of valuation—not administrative details after the purchase decision.

Investment grade: potentially attractive for disciplined long-horizon buyers; unsuitable for poorly capitalized speculation or buyers relying on gross yield, tax deductions or developer marketing alone.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Practical answers based on the July 2026 data cut-off and current official foreign-investment guidance.

Are Australian residential prices rising in 2026?
Yes in nominal terms at the national level, but the market is highly segmented. Official ABS stock-value data showed broad increases through the March quarter 2026, with Queensland and Western Australia strongest. Private estimates also showed Perth, Darwin and Brisbane outperforming Sydney and Melbourne.
Is the ABS mean dwelling price the same as a median sale price?
No. The ABS mean dwelling price is the estimated value of the dwelling stock divided by the number of dwellings. It is not a median transaction price and should not be used as a direct valuation for an individual property.
Can a foreigner buy an established house in Australia in 2026?
Generally no. Official guidance updated 1 July 2026 states that foreign investors are generally prohibited from purchasing established dwellings from 1 April 2025 to 30 June 2029, subject to limited exceptions.

Potentially yes, subject to approval or notification, property classification, developer exemption arrangements and all applicable fees and conditions. Independent legal advice is required before signing.
What gross rental yield is typical?
Global Property Guide estimated a national average gross yield of 4.69% in February 2026. Specific yields vary widely by city, suburb, property type and price. Net yields are materially lower after costs.
Which cities have the strongest recent growth?
Private early-2026 estimates showed Perth, Darwin and Brisbane among the strongest. Official state-level ABS data also showed strong increases in Western Australia and Queensland in the March quarter 2026.
Are Sydney and Melbourne poor investments?
Not necessarily. They offer deep employment, population and resale markets, but high entry prices and lower yields make asset selection and holding period more important. Melbourne may offer selective value but has state-tax and apartment-quality considerations.
What mortgage rates should an investor model?
RBA data for May 2026 showed average new investment housing loan rates of 6.39%, with principal-and-interest at 6.31% and interest-only at 6.49%. Actual rates vary and a higher stress-test rate should be used.
How much extra tax do foreign buyers pay?
It depends on the state. Official examples include 8% additional duty in Victoria and Queensland, and 7% in Western Australia and South Australia, in addition to ordinary transfer duty and federal approval fees. Annual foreign-owner land-tax surcharges may also apply.
Is short-term rental a better strategy than long-term rent?
Only in selected tourism and business locations after regulation, occupancy, management, cleaning, utilities, furnishing and insurance are fully modelled. There is no single national short-term rental regime.
What is the biggest apartment risk?
Building defects, weak owners-corporation finances, special levies, insurance exclusions and nearby competing supply can outweigh a higher headline yield. A strata records review is essential.
What is the most important pre-contract step?
Appoint an independent solicitor or conveyancer before signing and obtain written confirmation of buyer eligibility, duty, approval requirements, title, contract risks and due-diligence conditions.
