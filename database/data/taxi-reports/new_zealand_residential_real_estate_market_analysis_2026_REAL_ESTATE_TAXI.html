DIRECT ANALYSIS ANSWER

New Zealand entered mid-2026 as a buyer-favoured, regionally divided market rather than a broad national recovery.

The core 2026 signal is restraint: national modelled values remained below both the prior year and the early-2022 peak, sales activity weakened through the first five months of 2026, and listings gave buyers more choice. At the same time, lower mortgage rates than the 2023–24 peak, first-home-buyer participation and a strong rebound in dwelling consents support selected submarkets.

Auckland and Wellington continued to lag, while Hamilton, Christchurch and Dunedin showed firmer monthly movement in June. For investors, a headline gross yield near 4% is not automatically attractive when mortgage rates, insurance, local rates, maintenance, vacancy and management are included. For overseas buyers, legal eligibility must be confirmed before property selection; most non-residents still cannot freely buy ordinary residential property, although a new NZD 5 million-plus pathway exists for qualifying investor-visa holders.

NEW ZEALAND MARKET INTELLIGENCE

Data separation: official statistics, private estimates and analysis

A national report is reliable only when each statistic is labelled by source, date and methodology.

01

What the different data series measure

Official statistics: Stats NZ publishes building consents, dwelling and household estimates, rent indexes, population and economic data. The RBNZ publishes house-price indicators, mortgage rates, lending, LVR and DTI data. These series are authoritative but may be less timely than private market models.

Private market estimates: Cotality’s hedonic Home Value Index models value changes after controlling for property characteristics. REINZ publishes transaction-based median prices and sales measures. Global Property Guide aggregates selected source data and publishes cross-country yield estimates.

Asking prices and rents: portal data show seller or landlord expectations, not final agreed prices. A change in listing mix can move the median even when comparable property values are unchanged.

Real Estate Taxi analysis: the conclusions in this report interpret evidence and risk. They are not a registered valuation, legal opinion, tax ruling or investment guarantee.

Do not compare a modelled national value, a monthly transaction median and an asking price as if they were the same metric.
02

Executive market conclusion

  • National direction: soft to broadly flat, with Cotality’s June 2026 value measure down 0.9% year-on-year.
  • Buyer leverage: elevated choice and weaker sales volumes reduce urgency in many markets.
  • Stronger relative areas: Hamilton, Christchurch and Dunedin showed positive June movement, while Auckland and Wellington remained weak.
  • Supply: 39,737 new homes were consented in the year ended May 2026, a strong 19% annual increase, but consents are not the same as completed homes.
  • Investor hurdle: gross yields around 4% must be tested against mortgage, insurance, rates, maintenance and vacancy.
  • Foreign-buyer constraint: buyer eligibility is a legal gateway, not a minor administrative step.

The most defensible strategy is property-specific: buy below replacement-adjusted comparable value, in a liquid employment market, with verified insurance and a conservative net cash-flow model.

Prices, regional performance and market liquidity

Current private estimates show modest national weakness and significant divergence between major centres.

03

June 2026 national price direction

Cotality’s Home Value Index placed the national median property value at NZD 806,512 in June 2026. That was down 0.2% from May, down 0.8% over three months and down 0.9% from June 2025. The value remained 17.5% below the early-2022 peak of NZD 977,387.

04

Nominal versus inflation-adjusted performance

Global Property Guide’s February 2026 overview reported the RBNZ-linked Q4 2025 house-price measure at −0.73% nominal year-on-year and −3.73% after inflation. RBNZ key statistics showed CPI inflation at 3.1% year-on-year in the March 2026 quarter.

The real-return distinction is essential. A property can be almost flat in nominal dollars while losing purchasing power after inflation. Acquisition costs, maintenance, local rates, insurance, financing and tax increase the total return required to preserve capital.

The relevant hurdle for a leveraged investor is not CPI alone. Net rent plus realistic price growth must exceed financing and ownership costs over the full holding period.

06

Regional transaction medians: dated evidence, not current valuations

REINZ data reproduced by Global Property Guide showed a national median house price of NZD 786,977 in December 2025, up 1.4% year-on-year. Auckland was NZD 1,015,000, Canterbury NZD 725,000, Wellington NZD 770,000 and Waikato NZD 755,000.

These figures are useful transaction benchmarks but are dated and composition-sensitive. A December median can move because more expensive or cheaper homes sold that month. It should not be used as a July 2026 valuation without current comparable sales.

Use the regional table to identify broad affordability bands, then obtain recent same-suburb, same-property-type settled sales.
07

Sales volume and liquidity

Cotality’s June 2026 chart pack reported that May sales volumes fell 8.3% year-on-year, the fifth consecutive monthly decline. Across January–May 2026, sales activity was down 4.7%, representing 1,775 fewer transactions than the same period of 2025.

Lower turnover generally means longer marketing periods, more conditional offers and greater negotiation potential, but liquidity varies sharply. Standard family homes in established school zones can remain liquid while high-spec apartments, remote lifestyle property and unusual leasehold assets may take much longer to sell.

  • Measure comparable days on market, not only national sales counts.
  • Check withdrawn and relisted stock.
  • Ask how many genuinely competing offers existed.
  • Stress-test exit time in a weak market.
08

First-home buyers and investor demand

First-home buyers were the only major buyer group to increase purchase numbers in the first five months of 2026. Cotality reported 10,025 first-home-buyer transactions, up 2.4% year-on-year, and their market share rose from 25.8% to 27.7%.

This supports lower and middle price bands where borrowers can satisfy deposit and DTI constraints. It does not automatically support premium property, investor apartments or areas with weak employment growth.

Investor conditions improved after full interest deductibility returned from 1 April 2025, but investors still face ring-fencing of excess residential rental deductions and tighter LVR treatment than owner-occupiers.

09

Houses, townhouses and apartments

New Zealand’s multi-unit development cycle has expanded the supply of townhouses and attached homes. This can improve affordability and rental choice, but it creates micro-market oversupply risk where many near-identical units settle at once.

  • Detached houses: land component, broader family demand and redevelopment potential, but higher entry price and maintenance.
  • Townhouses: lower land content and modern compliance, but resale competition from new projects and body-corporate or shared-access issues.
  • Apartments: potentially higher gross yield and central access, but seismic, remediation, body-corporate, insurance and leasehold risks.

Compare land tenure, title type, body-corporate records, insurance and the competing development pipeline—not only bedroom count.

10

Foreign demand and migration sensitivity

Foreign-buyer restrictions mean international demand does not flow freely into ordinary residential property. Overseas participation is concentrated in buyers who meet citizenship or residence criteria, obtain consent, qualify under specific pathways or invest through permitted development and business structures.

Migration still matters indirectly through rental demand, household formation and labour markets. Falling net migration can soften rents in areas with high exposure to temporary workers or international students; a recovery can tighten those same markets quickly.

Do not treat “international interest” as equivalent to legally executable buyer demand.

Supply, construction and development risk

The 2026 consent rebound is important, but the pipeline must be converted into completions and absorbed by local demand.

11

Building consents: strong annual rebound

Stats NZ reported 39,737 new homes consented in the year ended May 2026, up 19% from the previous year. The seasonally adjusted number of new dwellings consented fell 4.0% in May after rising 11% in April.

Consents are a leading indicator, not completed supply. Projects can be delayed, redesigned, sold, cancelled or staged. Construction finance, presales, infrastructure capacity and builder solvency determine how much of the approved pipeline reaches occupation.

12

New-build versus existing-home economics

New builds offer modern insulation, lower near-term maintenance and compliance advantages, but buyers may pay a developer premium and face settlement valuation risk. Existing homes may offer land value and established neighbourhoods but can carry hidden moisture, seismic, electrical, plumbing and unconsented-work liabilities.

  • Compare new-build price with completed resales, not developer asking prices alone.
  • Verify code compliance certificate status and all permitted plans.
  • Check sunset clauses, deposit protection, variation rights and completion dates.
  • Allow for valuation shortfall between contract and settlement.
13

Off-plan and developer risk

Off-plan buyers face risks that do not exist in a completed property: insolvency, delay, specification substitution, changed views, smaller usable dimensions, body-corporate budgets based on optimistic assumptions and financing re-approval at settlement.

Use an independent solicitor experienced in development contracts. Confirm where the deposit is held, what security exists, whether the developer can cancel or extend, how defects are remedied and whether the buyer can assign the contract.

A lower initial deposit does not reduce the economic exposure. The buyer remains responsible for funding the full settlement price even if bank valuation or lending policy changes.
14

Vacancy and empty-property evidence

New Zealand does not have one universally accepted, high-frequency official national vacancy-rate series comparable across every city and property type. Private vacancy measures, rental listing duration, bond lodgements and local property-manager evidence should be triangulated.

Where reliable current data are unavailable, this report does not invent a national vacancy percentage. For a target property, request:

  • current comparable listings and days advertised;
  • recent signed rents, not asking rents;
  • seasonal vacancy history;
  • tenant profile and turnover;
  • planned competing supply.
NEW ZEALAND MARKET INTELLIGENCE

Rents, yields and landlord execution

Soft rental growth and high ownership costs make net-yield modelling more important than headline gross yield.

15

Long-term rents: latest dated national evidence

MBIE data reproduced by Global Property Guide placed the national mean weekly rent at NZD 568 for the year to September 2025, down 0.6% from the prior comparable period. Auckland averaged NZD 625, Wellington NZD 569, Canterbury NZD 513 and Otago NZD 574.

Trade Me’s October 2025 median asking rent was NZD 610, down 1.6% year-on-year. These measures differ: MBIE’s bond-linked data reflect actual tenancies, while portal asking rents reflect current listings.

Use current local signed-rent evidence before purchase. The dated national figures are context, not a July 2026 rent appraisal.

16

Gross yield versus realistic net yield

Global Property Guide estimated an average gross residential yield of 4.12% in January 2026, while Cotality’s contemporaneous estimate was about 3.8%. Gross yield is annual rent divided by purchase price before expenses.

A realistic net model deducts management, vacancy, letting, rates, insurance, body-corporate levies, repairs, compliance, accounting, furnishing and capital replacements. Debt service must then be added separately.

At a 4.1% gross yield, a property financed with debt priced above 5% can be materially cash-flow negative before principal repayment.
17

Short-term rental and holiday-home strategy

Short-stay performance is highly local and seasonal. Tourism markets such as Queenstown, Rotorua, Auckland and parts of coastal New Zealand can achieve strong peak rates, but occupancy, council rules, visitor accommodation standards, GST treatment, insurance and private-use apportionment can materially change the result.

  • Confirm district-plan and body-corporate permission.
  • Model shoulder and winter seasons, not only peak weeks.
  • Include platform, cleaning, linen, utilities, management and furnishing replacement.
  • Compare after-tax net return with a long-term tenancy.
18

Tenancy law and rent increases

Tenancy Services states that a landlord must generally give at least 60 days’ written notice of a rent increase. The increase can take effect only after 12 months from the tenancy start and 12 months after the previous increase took effect.

Fixed-term and periodic tenancies have different termination mechanics. Healthy Homes standards, smoke alarms, bond handling, records and maintenance obligations should be costed into operations.

Use current Tenancy Services guidance and a competent local property manager. Do not base returns on non-compliant rent increases or unrealistic possession assumptions.

Mortgages, credit rules and affordability

Lower rates than the recent peak support buyers, but July 2026 policy tightening and serviceability rules keep leverage constrained.

20

LVR and DTI restrictions

RBNZ macroprudential rules limit the share of high-risk lending rather than prohibiting every individual loan. Current guidance classifies investor lending above a 70% LVR as high-LVR and restricts the share of such lending. DTI speed limits allow only a portion of new lending above a DTI of 6 for owner-occupiers and above 7 for investors.

  • Investor deposits are generally expected to be larger.
  • Bank approval can be stricter than the regulatory minimum.
  • Rental income may be shaded in serviceability calculations.
  • New-build and construction lending may be treated differently.

Obtain written pre-approval and confirm the exact property is acceptable to the lender before the agreement becomes unconditional.

21

Affordability and buyer depth

Households face a combination of high price-to-income ratios, deposit requirements, mortgage testing rates, insurance costs and local rates. The post-2021 price correction improved some ratios, but affordability remains restrictive in Auckland, Queenstown and other expensive markets.

For investors, affordability affects both entry and exit. A property above local owner-occupier borrowing capacity needs deeper investor, migration, tourism or wealth-buyer demand. Premium assets with weak rent support can be highly sensitive to confidence and credit availability.

Stress-test a higher interest rate, lower rent, six months of vacancy and a delayed resale. If the investment fails under moderate stress, the purchase price or leverage is too high.
22

Currency and non-resident finance risk

Buyers earning income outside New Zealand can face currency translation, foreign-income verification and lender-policy risk. A fall in the buyer’s home currency against NZD raises the real cost of deposit, repayments and operating expenses.

Separate the property thesis from the currency thesis. A good property investment can still produce a poor home-currency result, and currency gains should not be treated as guaranteed return.

Legal eligibility to buy, immigration status and bank finance are separate tests. Passing one does not guarantee the others.

NEW ZEALAND MARKET INTELLIGENCE

Overseas buyers, taxes and purchase procedure

Eligibility, title, tax and natural-hazard due diligence must be resolved before an offer becomes unconditional.

23

Who can buy residential property

In general, New Zealand citizens can buy residential property, and residence-class visa holders may buy if they meet the applicable commitment-to-reside criteria. Most overseas people cannot freely buy ordinary homes and may require Overseas Investment Office consent or may be ineligible.

Citizens of Australia and Singapore have special treatment under New Zealand’s international obligations, but eligibility depends on the person’s exact status and the type of land. Sensitive land, large landholdings and business assets can trigger additional rules.

Use the LINZ homebuyer eligibility tool and obtain written advice from a New Zealand lawyer before signing. The sale agreement itself requires a residential land statement.
24

NZD 5 million-plus investor-visa pathway

From March 2026, qualifying holders of Active Investor Plus, Investor 1 or Investor 2 visas can apply to buy or build one residential property worth more than NZD 5 million, subject to the Overseas Investment Act pathway and conditions.

This is not a general reopening of the housing market to all foreign buyers. The visa category, property value, ownership entity, consent, occupation and ongoing compliance must all be checked. A premium price alone does not create eligibility.

Use current LINZ guidance because application forms, fees and evidence requirements can change.

25

Purchase costs and annual ownership costs

New Zealand does not generally impose a broad stamp duty on residential purchases, but buyers still incur meaningful transaction costs. Budget for lawyer or conveyancer, title search, LIM, building inspection, valuation, finance, specialist engineering, insurance and moving or connection costs.

Annual ownership costs commonly include council rates, insurance, maintenance, body-corporate levies, management, accounting, Healthy Homes compliance and capital replacements. Leasehold property also requires ground-rent and review analysis.

Low entry taxes do not mean low friction. A short holding period can still be uneconomic after selling commission, legal fees, maintenance and price volatility.
26

Income tax, bright-line and rental deductions

For residential property sold on or after 1 July 2024, the bright-line test generally applies when the bright-line end date falls within two years of the start date, subject to main-home and other exclusions. Sales outside the period can still be taxable under intention, pattern, dealer, developer or builder rules.

From 1 April 2025, qualifying residential rental interest is again 100% deductible under the general rules. Excess residential rental deductions remain ring-fenced and generally cannot offset salary or wages.

Offshore persons can be subject to Residential Land Withholding Tax when a taxable sale occurs. Rental income is taxable, and GST can become relevant for short-stay or development activity.

Obtain transaction-specific New Zealand tax advice before purchase, restructuring, subdivision, change of use or sale.

27

Standard buying procedure

  1. Confirm legal eligibility and obtain finance pre-assessment.
  2. Appoint an independent lawyer or conveyancer before offering.
  3. Review title, easements, covenants, LIM, zoning, hazards and council files.
  4. Commission building, moisture, seismic or engineering inspections as appropriate.
  5. Review insurance availability and indicative premium before going unconditional.
  6. Negotiate a written sale and purchase agreement with finance, title, LIM, inspection and other conditions where required.
  7. Satisfy conditions, complete final inspection and settle through the lawyer.

Auction bids are generally unconditional. Complete due diligence and finance before bidding.

28

Title, zoning, consent and access risk

A record of title can reveal mortgages, easements, covenants, cross-lease interests, leasehold tenure and access obligations. A LIM summarises council-held information but does not replace a title search, building inspection or full property file.

  • Match existing structures to approved plans and code compliance records.
  • Verify boundaries, shared driveways, retaining walls and services.
  • Review district-plan zoning and possible intensification.
  • Check heritage, designation, coastal and natural-hazard overlays.
  • For cross-lease, confirm the flats plan matches current improvements.
29

Building condition, weathertightness and unit-title risk

New Zealand has material building-condition risks, including historic leaky-building defects, untreated timber, borer, asbestos, earthquake-prone buildings, deferred maintenance and unconsented alterations. A generic visual inspection may not be enough.

For apartments and unit titles, review body-corporate minutes, long-term maintenance plan, seismic reports, insurance, claims, levies, litigation, remediation history and proposed special levies. For leasehold apartments, model future ground-rent reviews.

A discounted apartment can be expensive if insurance, seismic strengthening or façade remediation is unresolved.
30

Earthquake, flood, coastal and insurance risk

Natural-hazard exposure is a core part of New Zealand property value. Earthquake shaking, liquefaction, landslip, flooding, tsunami, volcanic and coastal-erosion risks differ street by street. Climate adaptation decisions can also affect infrastructure, insurability and lender appetite.

  • Check council hazard maps and the LIM.
  • Review Natural Hazards Commission claim history where available.
  • Obtain an insurance quote before going unconditional.
  • Confirm excesses, exclusions and replacement basis.
  • For coastal property, examine erosion, inundation and access resilience.

A property that cannot obtain adequate insurance may also be difficult to finance and resell.

NEW ZEALAND MARKET INTELLIGENCE

Investor fit, opportunities, risks and two-year scenarios

The market can reward patient, selective buyers, but not every property benefits from the same national drivers.

31

Where opportunity may exist

  • Established family homes in diversified employment centres bought below recent comparable value.
  • Christchurch and selected South Island markets with relative affordability and firmer 2026 momentum.
  • Well-located townhouses where competing pipeline is limited and body-corporate or shared-access obligations are clear.
  • Value-add existing homes with verified consent pathway and realistic renovation budget.
  • Premium properties for legally eligible buyers only when scarcity, insurance and resale depth are demonstrated.

Opportunity should come from price, property quality or execution—not a vague assumption that the whole country will rebound.

32

Who may not be suited to this market

  • Highly leveraged investors relying on rapid price appreciation.
  • Foreign buyers who have not confirmed legal eligibility.
  • Short-term flippers exposed to selling costs and tax rules.
  • Buyers unable to absorb insurance, rates or special-levy shocks.
  • Investors purchasing generic new townhouses in oversupplied micro-markets.
  • Anyone treating a 4% gross yield as 4% spendable cash return.
New Zealand property is most suitable for buyers with a long horizon, conservative leverage and the ability to perform asset-specific legal and technical due diligence.

34

Practical due-diligence checklist

  • Confirm citizenship, residence or OIO pathway in writing.
  • Obtain finance approval for the exact property and title type.
  • Analyse recent settled comparable sales.
  • Review title, easements, covenants, cross-lease or leasehold terms.
  • Obtain LIM, council property file and zoning/hazard review.
  • Complete independent building and moisture inspection.
  • Check code compliance and all alterations.
  • Secure insurance terms before unconditional commitment.
  • Review body-corporate records and long-term maintenance plan.
  • Model gross and net rent with vacancy and capital expenditure.
  • Check tenancy, Healthy Homes and short-stay compliance.
  • Obtain tax advice on ownership structure, bright-line, rental income and GST.
  • Stress-test rates, rent, vacancy, special levies and resale timing.
  • Use an independent lawyer, not only seller or developer documents.
FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

New Zealand residential property market FAQ

Answers use the data and rules available at the 16 July 2026 cut-off.

Are New Zealand house prices rising in 2026?
Not nationally in a strong or uniform way. Cotality's June 2026 modelled national median value was NZD 806,512, down 0.2% month-on-month and 0.9% year-on-year, with large regional differences.
Can overseas buyers buy residential property in New Zealand?
Generally only New Zealand citizens and qualifying residents can buy homes without restriction. A special pathway introduced in March 2026 allows qualifying investor-visa holders to buy or build a residential property worth more than NZD 5 million, subject to detailed conditions and consent requirements.
What is the New Zealand bright-line period in 2026?
For residential property sold on or after 1 July 2024, the bright-line test generally examines whether the sale occurs within two years of acquisition, subject to exclusions and other land-sale tax rules.
What mortgage rates were advertised in June 2026?
RBNZ's standard advertised averages included 6.15% floating, 5.28% for one year, 5.69% for two years and 6.17% for five years. Actual special rates can be lower and depend on borrower conditions.
What are the main property risks in New Zealand?
Major risks include earthquake and liquefaction exposure, flooding and coastal hazards, insurance availability, weathertightness defects, unconsented work, unit-title liabilities, oversupply in some townhouse markets and weak net yield after costs.
Is New Zealand property suitable for a short-term investor?
Usually not without a clear edge. Transaction friction, financing costs, tax rules and price volatility favour buyers with a longer holding period, conservative leverage and strong property-specific due diligence.
